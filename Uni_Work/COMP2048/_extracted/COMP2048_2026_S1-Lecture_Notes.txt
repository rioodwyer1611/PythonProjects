Overview

What is the physicality of computation? The possibility that we can leverage
quantum mechanics to do computation in new and interesting ways could,
theoretically, form the basis of a very powerful form of computation. These
set of notes are to company an introduction to quantum computing in three
lectures for COMP2048. The main ideas we will cover are presented in
introductory quantum computing courses for non-physicists.

Quantum computers as Turing machines

In 1936, Alan Turing described an abstract model of computation, called a
Turing machine, that we would now recognise as a modern, general-purpose
programmable computer. He claimed that any algorithmic task could be
performed by a Universal Turing Machine, and this machine could simulate
any other Turing machine.
The next several decades saw rapid advancements in the development
of physical hardware that could perform generally programmable computations. A leading hardware platform materialized whereby electronic switches
represented discrete bits of information. This computing hardware could be
contrasted with analogue computing, for example. In any case, circuitbased hardware for electronic computations improved a pace that has come
to be called Moore’s law, whereby computer power doubled for constant cost
roughly once every two years [1], at least until very recently.
Now however, physical systems for electronic computation appears to
be limited in the sense that further reductions in size may not be accessible without having to take into account the physics of small systems i.e.
quantum mechanical effects. Perhaps we could adjust our design or engineering calculations to accommodate any errors arising from treating a system according to classical physics even though its behavior might be better
described quantum mechanically.
Or perhaps we might be able to process information - not according
to the classical physics of our everyday lives - but instead according to new
physical rules that only apply when systems become very small (and reasonably well isolated) from their surroundings. Could current limits on physical
computation be overcome by switching to an entirely new paradigm of computing? Addressing this tantalizing question is at the heart of theoretical
and experimental quantum computing today.
For our purposes, we will informally define such a device where its components behave according to the rules of quantum mechanics.
Definition 1 (A quantum computer). A quantum computer is a device that
uses the principles of quantum mechanics to perform computation.
Notice that any such quantum computing device is still describable as
a Turing machine. This correspondence between the evolution of a quantum system and a Universal Turing Machine was first outlined in the 1980s,
where Ref. [8] argued that a computation that could be mapped to quantum mechanical states and operators is equivalent to a Turing machine of
equivalent or greater power [2]. The first definition of quantum Turing ma3

chines (QTMs) was provided in 1985 by David Deutsch [9], where such a
machine has an infinite tape, a transition function, and the actions of the
machine are local and completely specified by this transition function. However, quantum Turing machines have two additional departures: first, the
machine’s evolution is always time reversible, and secondly, at each step,
transition probabilities can interfere, i.e. amplify or cancel out, according
to the rules of quantum mechanics. Subsequently, it was shown that QTMs
are discrete machines even though transition probabilities are real valued
and interference is typically thought of in terms of continuous behavior [10].
Since we know that quantum computation can be seen as Turing machines, we know that quantum computing conforms to the Church-Turing
conjecture, i.e. from Week 5’s lectures,
Definition 2 (Church-Turing Thesis CTT). Any function that can be computed by an algorithm can be computed by a Turing Machine.
This statement about computability. However, the discovery of certain
quantum algorithms appear to challenge the Extended Church-Turing Thesis, which is statement about efficiency of computation.
Definition 3 (Extended Church-Turing Thesis ECTT). Any algorithmic
process can be simulated efficiently using a Probabilistic Turing Machine.
[2]
This thesis asserts that any reasonable computational model can be simulated efficiently by a probabilistic Turing machine. Here, efficiency means
the simulation overhead is bounded by a polynomial. You may have already
heard that that the class of problems solvable in polynomial time by classical
computers is P (deterministic bits) or BP P (random bits). It is known that
P ⊆ BP P , but it is unknown if P = BP P . The ECTT conjectures that all
physically realizable computations belong to BP P . The implication is that
no physical machine operating according to the rules of everyday physics,
i.e. classical computers, can solve problems much faster than probabilistic
Turing machines.
But what do we mean by ‘physically realizable algorithmic process’ ?
What if the computers that we build do not operate according to the rules
of classical physics? What if we instead use the rules of quantum physics to
process information? Remarkably in the 1980s and 1990s, it was found that
algorithms that processed information according to the rules of quantum
physics could solve certain problems exponentially faster than algorithms
behaving according to the rules of classical physics. Some of these early

Quantum and classical complexity

A summary of complexity classes is provided in Fig. 1 summarising what is
known, as well as open questions in the field.
Classically, Fig. 1a depicts known relationships between classical complexity classes discussed in Ref. [13], where each line depicts containments
going upwards. I will not define or discuss each complexity class but I will
informally summarise them as follows. The class P is the class of computational problems that can be solved quickly on a classical computer. N P
is the class of problems which have solutions which can be quickly checked
on a classical computer. N P -complete problems are ‘at least as hard’ as all
other problems in N P . PSPACE consists of those problems which can be

solved using resources which are few in spatial size but not necessarily in
time. Currently there is no proof that P = N P . If P ̸= N P , then no N P complete problem can be efficiently solved on a classical computer. Finally,
the complexity class BP P is the class of problems that can be solved using
randomized algorithms in polynomial time with a bounded probability of
error. BP P is widely regarded as being the class of problems which should
be considered efficiently solvable on a classical computer. It is not known if
BP P ⊆ N P .
Quantumly, Fig. 1b depicts a number of quantum complexity classes, but
we will only discuss BQP . Informally, BQP is the class of all computational
problems which can be solved efficiently on a quantum computer with a
bounded probability of error. It is considered to be an analogue of BP P .
The key point is that where BQP contains P , BP P but it’s relationship to
N P is as yet unknown.
Can we prove that quantum computers are more efficient than classical
computers? This statement would require the proof that BP P ̸= BQP .
From Fig. 1b, we notice that P ⊆ BP P ⊆ BQP ⊆ P P ⊆ P SP ACE.
Proving that BP P ̸= BQP implies that P ̸=PSPACE which is as hard as
proving P ̸= N P . If it is proved that quantum computers are strictly more
powerful than classical computers, then it will follow that P is not equal to
PSPACE.
Note that while containments between classes are known, i.e. , P ⊆
BP P ⊆ BQP ⊆ P P ⊆ P SP ACE, we don’t yet know if these classes increase or are exactly equivalent. Consider, for example, BP P ⊆ BQP .
Here, factoring is believed to be in BQP but hoped not to be in BP P . This
conjecture forms the basis of RSA encryption techniques. Specifically, factoring is not (currently known to be) NP-complete and it is believed to be in
N P but not in P . Indeed, it is not known whether quantum computers can
be used to quickly solve all the problems in N P . A introductory discussion
of quantum complexity classes can be found in Refs. [2, 1] and a detailed
discussion can be found in [13].

Quantum postulates

A postulate is a statement that is accepted as true without proof and used
as a starting assumption for constructing theories or mathematical systems.
Quantum theory refers to a set of postulates or rules by which we describe
the behaviour of physical systems. We observe that physical systems behave
according to the rules of quantum mechanics when a physical system is very
well-isolated from the rest of the universe. Physically, we can achieve this
in special laboratories where we carefully observe physical systems that are
e.g. very small, very cold, and/or in-vacuum. (Analogously, Einstein’s rules
of special relativity don’t apply to an observer at rest, but only to observers
that are moving close to the speed of light.) A quantum algorithm processes
information according to the rules of quantum mechanics in order to solve
particular problem.
Exercise: Consider a coin in your hand. Why doesn’t this coin behave
quantum mechanically?
Let’s now go through each of the postulates that will tell you what these
rules are, and how we use them to design quantum algorithms. Informally,
the goal of a quantum algorithm is to prepare a quantum state (Postulate
1) using transformations (Postulate 2) that when this state is measured
(Postulate 3), the observed outcomes will be sampled from a probability
distribution that will solve a task with high degree of certainty. When we
scale the size of the problem to many quantum systems (Postulate 4), then
quantum advantage is achieved if there is no classical problem that can
efficiently solve the same problem.

Postulate 1: Quantum states

Under one interpretation of quantum mechanics, a quantum state contains
all information about a physical system to derive probabilities for measurements performed on the system.
Postulate 1 (Quantum states.). A quantum state contains all information
about a physical system to derive probabilities for measurements performed
on the system. We cannot directly observe a quantum state but it provides
a description from which probability distributions of any measured outcomes
can be derived.
A remarkable assertion in quantum mechanics is that all possible information about physically measurable properties of a quantum system are
contained in this abstract quantum state. The quantum state contains information about the probabilities of all possible outcomes we can observe if
we were to perform experiments. We cannot directly observe the quantum
state, but only compute probabilities of observations from the information
contained within it.
While sounding mysterious, a quantum state is represented concretely
by mathematical objects. Mathematically, we say that if our system or a
machine has a finite number of discrete states N , then our quantum state
will be a N -dimensional complex unit vector.
Why do we need to use complex numbers to describe quantum states?
Well in other areas of engineering and science, complex numbers help us
to describe the behavior of waves (sound waves, water waves etc.) and
phenomena like superpositions of waves and wave interference.
However, in quantum mechanics, we use complex numbers to describe the
interference and superpositions of quantum states of a system. Physically, it
implies that the behavior of fundamental particles we observe in experiments
breaks the distinction between waves and particles, e.g. an electron can
behave both like a wave or a particle.

In this subsection, we describe how to transform quantum states to other
valid quantum states. What this means is that you can break up your
quantum algorithm into a sequential series of logical operations you want to
perform on your input state. If a quantum state is represented by a vector,
you might have guessed that these quantum operations will be presented by
a matrix.
Before we go further, let us remind ourselves what a matrix is.

Universality of quantum computing

In classical computing we have a set of commonly used gates: AND, NOT,
OR, NAND, XOR, FANOUT, etc. We can use combinations of these gates
to perform any computation in classical computing. A classical computer
that can run these gates is Turing-complete or universal.
Is there a gate or set of gates that are universal for quantum computing?
In fact, there are several combinations of gate sets that lead to universality. Of these, the gate set {CX, T, S} is universal and consists of 2 single
qubit gates, and 1 two qubit gate, from which any N -qubit circuit can perform computation. For each hardware platform, a quantum computer will
natively (physically) implement some kind of two-qubit interaction. This
physical interaction is then converted to a logical CX gate by pre- or postmultiplying the interaction with single-qubit gates.
There are two other gate sets that are worth mentioning in quantum
computing. The first is the Clifford group, generated by {CX, H, S}. It
is known that any computation decomposable into elements of the Clifford
group. The second is the Pauli group, which is the name given to the set
of single qubit gates {I, X, Y, Z}. The two groups are related since for a
Clifford operator C and any Pauli P , the product C † P C gives you another
Pauli operator Q. Notice that multiplying any two Pauli gates gives you
another Pauli gate, so the Pauli group is a subset of the Clifford group.
Any quantum computation that contains only Clifford group elements is
efficiently classically simulable. To make the Clifford group more interesting,
we add a T gate, and it is known that the Clifford + T group is a universal
gate set. As an illustration, I’ve written some code so that you can see
how one can rotate a |0⟩ (green) to other pure states (blue points) through
random sequences of varying lengths using only H and T gates (see Fig. 3).
A specific single qubit gate can indeed be decomposed into H and T gates
to arbitrary precision but these sequences will not be random.
In the next section, we introduce quantum measurements. Unlike quantum gates we have just discussed, measurements represent irreversible (non
unitary) transformations of quantum states.

4.5

Random classical algorithm

Notice that the classical query cost is expensive only if we require absolutely
perfect performance. If a classical procedure queries f in randomly then, in
the case of a balanced function, it would have to be very unlucky to always
draw the same bit value.
Suppose a random classical algorithm makes k queries randomly (independently) and then outputs “constant” if all queries are the same, or
“balanced” otherwise. If f is constant, then we will be correct with 100%
probability. If f is balanced, then we will be wrong with some probability
ϵ.
Suppose I make k random queries. Let the first query be my reference.
Then I would have to get the same output on k − 1 queries for me to
incorrectly identify a true balanced function as being constant.
What is the probability that the classical algorithm fails? Well it’s a
probability of seeing the same output k − 1 times, and seeing a different
outcome on the k-query. The probability of seeing the same outcome k − 1
times is multiplicative, 12 × 21 × . . . × 12 , which gives me a total probability of
error of ϵ = ( 12 )k−1 . The error probability for a random classical algorithm
dies off pretty quickly as you increase the number of random classical queries
k.
Exercise: Suppose one solves the constant vs. balance problem using
a classical random algorithm and we are willing to tolerate ϵ error. Show
that the number of classical queries required is now O(log2 ( 1ϵ )). What can
we conclude about quantum advantage for the constant vs. balanced oracle
problem?
